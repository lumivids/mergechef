Libya is almost the size of Western Europe. Ninety-five percent of it is desert. It has no permanent rivers. Not one.

Yet when people in Tripoli turn on the tap, water comes out. And most of that water has traveled more than a thousand kilometers under the Sahara — through pipes so wide you could drive a car inside them.

So how do you build a river where it never rains? And where does the water actually come from?

Let's dig into the largest underground water network ever built.

Almost every Libyan lives on a thin strip along the Mediterranean coast. For centuries, they drank from shallow wells near the sea.

But as cities like Tripoli and Benghazi grew, those wells were pumped harder and harder. And when you pull fresh water out of a coastal aquifer too fast, the sea moves in to fill the gap.

Think of a sponge sitting at the edge of a bathtub. Squeeze out the fresh water, and it soaks up whatever is next to it — in this case, salt water.

By the 1970s, wells were turning salty. Farms were dying. The country needed a new source of water — fast.

The obvious answer was the sea itself. Libya has nearly two thousand kilometers of coastline. Why not just remove the salt, like Saudi Arabia does?

Because back then, desalination was brutally expensive. Ripping salt out of seawater burns enormous amounts of energy, and building enough plants to supply an entire country would have cost a fortune — every single year, forever. Libya needed something cheaper. Something that, once built, would simply flow.

The answer had been found decades earlier — by accident. In the 1950s, oil companies were drilling deep in the southern desert, looking for petroleum. Instead, some wells hit something else.

Water. Enormous amounts of it.

Beneath the sand lies the Nubian Sandstone Aquifer, one of the largest reserves of fresh groundwater on the planet. It stretches under Libya, Egypt, Sudan and Chad.

And this water is old. Very old. It fell as rain when the Sahara was green — a land of lakes, grasslands and giraffes, thousands of years ago. When the climate changed and the desert took over, that rainwater stayed trapped in the rock.

It's not a lake. It's more like a giant stone sponge soaked with ancient rain.

There was just one problem. The water was in the deep south. The people were on the north coast. Between them: more than a thousand kilometers of the hottest desert on Earth.

There were two options. Move the people to the water — or move the water to the people.

Moving farmers and cities into the deep Sahara had already been tried, and it was too expensive to keep people alive out there. So in 1984, Libya chose the second option: carry the water north.

Not by truck. Not by canal — an open canal in the Sahara would lose a huge share of its water to evaporation before it ever reached the coast.

The water would travel underground, inside a sealed pipe network. Libya's leader, Muammar Gaddafi, called it "the Eighth Wonder of the World."

And the bill was staggering — tens of billions of dollars. Most megaprojects of this size are built with huge foreign loans. Libya paid for it largely with the one thing it had in endless supply: oil money. The plan was simple. Sell the black liquid under the desert, and use the cash to pump out the clear one.

The main construction work went to a South Korean company, and thousands of engineers and laborers from around the world moved into camps in the middle of the Sahara — places where the nearest town could be hundreds of kilometers away.

To move that much water, ordinary pipes were useless. The engineers needed pipes four meters wide — taller than a basketball hoop.

Each section is about seven and a half meters long and weighs as much as a fully loaded truck — around seventy-five tons. And the project needed hundreds of thousands of them.

You can't import hundreds of thousands of concrete giants across the ocean. So Libya built the factories in the desert itself.

Each pipe starts as a steel cylinder. Workers pour concrete around it, then wrap it tightly in high-strength steel wire — like winding a rubber band around a can, over and over. That wire squeezes the concrete so hard that, when water pressure pushes outward, the pipe pushes back.

Making the pipes was only half the battle. Now they had to cross hundreds of kilometers of sand.

Special trucks were built to carry a single pipe at a time. Convoys rolled day and night across the desert, delivering section after section.

Then came the trenches — around seven meters deep. Giant cranes lowered each pipe into place, and crews joined them end to end with sealed joints, then buried everything under the sand.

From the surface, you'd see almost nothing. But beneath the dunes, a concrete river was growing, kilometer by kilometer.

At the other end, the water still had to be pulled out of the rock.

Engineers drilled well fields in the deep south — at places like Sarir, Tazerbo and Kufra — and in the Fezzan for the western line. In total, more than a thousand wells, many of them hundreds of meters deep.

At the bottom of each well, a powerful electric pump lifts the water to the surface and pushes it into collector pipes. Hundreds of wells feed into one main artery — like small streams joining to form a river. Only this river flows north, under the sand, and it was made by humans.

Water flows downhill. But the route north isn't flat, and some cities sit higher than the wells.

So the system uses a mix of gravity and muscle. Where the land slopes down, gravity does the work for free. Where it rises, pumping stations push the water up and over.

Along the way, the water is stored in enormous reservoirs — some holding millions of cubic meters. They act like batteries for water: they fill up when supply is high and release water when cities need it most.

In 1991, the first phase was finished. For the first time in history, water from the deep Sahara flowed out of taps on the Mediterranean coast.

And the scale is hard to imagine. At full capacity, the network was designed to move millions of cubic meters of water every single day. That's like filling more than a thousand Olympic swimming pools — every day — with water pulled from under the driest place on the continent.

Not all of it went to drinking. Out in the desert, near the well fields, giant green circles began to appear — farms irrigated by rotating sprinklers, so large and so perfectly round that you can spot them from space. Wheat and vegetables, growing in the middle of the Sahara.

But concrete and steel buried in the desert have a weakness.

Remember that steel wire squeezing each pipe? If water and salt reach it, the wire slowly rusts. And a pipe under enormous pressure, with its wire rusting away inside, can suddenly burst — tearing open the sand and flooding the desert.

In the early years, some pipes did exactly that. Engineers started listening to the pipes — using acoustic monitoring to hear the tiny snap of individual wires breaking, so damaged sections could be replaced before they exploded.

And here's the deeper irony. The Great Man-Made River was sold as a river. But a river refills itself every year with rain. This one doesn't.

The water in the Nubian aquifer fell thousands of years ago. Almost nothing is replacing it today. Every glass of water from these pipes is a little piece of an ancient climate that will never come back.

Libya isn't harvesting a river. It's slowly spending a fossil.

Think of it like a savings account left by a grandparent you never met. You can live very comfortably on it for a long time. But nobody is making deposits anymore. And every withdrawal brings you one step closer to zero.

Saudi Arabia learned this lesson the hard way. In the 1980s, it pumped its own ancient groundwater to grow wheat in the desert — and within a few decades, much of that water was gone. Libya is drinking from the same kind of account.

Then came the war.

After 2011, the system was caught in Libya's conflict. A pipe factory at Brega was hit by an airstrike. Wells in the south were vandalized and stripped for parts. Power cuts shut down pumps for days at a time.

And that reveals how fragile the whole design is. Millions of people now depend on a handful of pipelines running through empty desert. Damage one key point, and cities hundreds of kilometers away can lose water.

A country with no rivers built one artificial river — and then tied its survival to it.

So how long can it last?

Nobody knows exactly. Some studies suggest the aquifer holds enough water for centuries. Others warn that local water levels are already dropping, and that wells will need to go deeper and deeper — costing more energy every year.

Meanwhile, the pipes are getting older, and much of the original network has passed thirty years of service.

The Great Man-Made River proved that humans can move a sea of water across the largest hot desert on Earth. What it can't do is bring back the rain that filled it.

Every time someone in Tripoli fills a glass of water, they're drinking rain that fell when the Sahara was green — carried more than a thousand kilometers through a concrete tunnel.

So here's the question: if you knew the water coming out of your tap could never be replaced... would you still call it a river? Or is it a countdown?
